Posting a job is free, so Handshake does not sell access. It sells reach and screening efficiency: more messages, better filters, promotion, ranked applicants, analytics. The flywheel — and exactly where it breaks

More students›Employers get qualified applicants›Employers upgrade / renew›More & better jobs posted›Students get real outcomes›Career centers promote Handshake›More students
Break 1 — congestion

Better matching sends everyone to the same "best" jobs. Those roles get 800 applications, close early, and 790 job seekers get nothing. Aggregate CTR looks great.

Break 2 — ghosting

Free unlimited posting plus one-click apply produces volume without reciprocity. Job seekers who never hear back stop returning, which silently degrades supply.

Break 3 — thin markets

Rural schools, niche majors, and specific geographies have structurally too little demand. No ranking model fixes an empty market; that needs supply seeding.

How to run the 30 minutes

1. Ask before you frame

Don't propose a KPI in the first two minutes. Ask what's already being measured, what prompted this conversation, and what decision the KPI would actually drive.

2. Say the tradeoff out loud

Every KPI on a job-creation flow has a speed-vs-quality tension. Naming it unprompted is worth more than picking the "right" metric.

3. Treat the PM as a co-owner

This is collaborative, not adversarial. Build on their framing, ask what they've already tried, and check your assumptions against what they know about the product.

4. Close with one recommendation

Not three options with no opinion. State the KPI pairing (or the next test) you'd actually ship, and why.

Part 1 — Employer Job-Creation product: KPIs, diagnostics, guardrails

The Job-Creation product is the flow an employer goes through to post a role: title, description, requirements, screening questions, salary/location, review, publish — optionally assisted by AI drafting tools or an ATS/XML feed. It's the entry point to every downstream number in the business: no completed job creation, no applications, no qualified applicants, no renewal, no Promoted Jobs upsell.

Clarifying questions to ask first
  • Are we scoping just the creation flow (title → publish), or does "success" extend into what happens after — applications and qualified applicants on that job?
  • Is the concern more about volume of postings, quality of postings, or speed/friction to publish? Those point to different KPIs.
  • Are we optimizing for the free-tier employer, the paid-tier employer, or both — their incentives to finish the flow carefully are different.
  • Is there a specific pain point already observed — high abandonment, jobs that get published but sit empty, or complaints about the flow being slow?
  • Does this include AI-assisted drafting and ATS/XML-fed postings, or just the manual, human-typed flow?
The 1–2 KPIs — connecting user value to business value

Pick a pair, not a single number — one alone is gameable. Present them as a package: the flow-level KPI measures whether the product respects the employer's time; the outcome-level KPI measures whether what came out of the flow was worth their time.

Why you need both, said as one sentence

"A flow that maximizes completion rate alone can do it by deleting friction that was doing useful work — like requiring a salary band or clear requirements — so I'd never report completion without qualified-application rate sitting right next to it."

Coverage caveat — say this before anyone catches it
  • "Advanced" = a status change (New → Reviewing/Interview), not just opened/viewed.
  • Handshake can only see that status change in 2 cases: recruiter reviews inside Handshake, or the employer's ATS sends status write-back.
  • No integration, or ATS-redirect apply with no write-back → zero visibility after submit.
  • Fix: exclude those unobserved applications from the denominator — don't count them as "rejected."
  • Always report a coverage % next to the rate, e.g. "34%, over the 61% of applications we can observe."

The trap question: "which side matters more?"
Don't pick a side — name which one is scarcer
  • Recruiter-side metrics are the revenue-linked ones, so it's tempting to say employers matter more. That's the trap.
  • Job-seeker attention is the scarcer resource — it's what Handshake actually sells to employers, and it's finite. Every promoted impression and every ranking change is really an allocation of it.
  • Recruiter happiness can be bought at the job seeker's expense: a ranking change can concentrate good candidates onto fewer postings, which looks like a recruiter win and a marketplace loss.
  • Worse, stack-ranking job seekers by experience pushes fresh graduates down — the exact population the mission exists for. That's why the Zero-experience advance-rate parity guardrail sits in Core Metrics.
  • The line to say: "Employers pay, so they're the revenue side — but job-seeker attention is the inventory we're selling. I'd protect the scarcer side, because a recruiter-side win funded by job-seeker churn shows up in revenue two renewal cycles later."

Part 2 (if time allows) — diagnosing Promoted-Job Upsell Rate below industry average

Same four-step shape as any metric diagnosis: validate the comparison before trusting it, segment, rule out causes in order of cheapness to check, and name the next data pull or test — not a conclusion.

What Promoted Jobs actually does
  • Employer pays a daily budget for a specific job.
  • That job then gets shown more often and in better positions in job seeker feeds and search results than it would rank organically.
  • It's an auction/impression-based mechanic — think "boosted post," not "better matching."
Promoted-Job Upsell Rate — formula

Accounts that paid to promote ≥1 job in the period ÷ accounts that posted ≥1 job in that same period.

Step 1 — Validate the comparison before reacting to it
  • Confirm the exact definition on both sides: numerator = accounts purchasing Promoted Jobs in period; denominator = eligible accounts (posted ≥1 job) in the same period. Get the benchmark source's definition in the same terms.
  • Handshake's Enterprise tier bundles Promoted Jobs in for free — confirmed on their own pricing page.
  • Those Enterprise accounts can never appear in our upsell numerator, since they never separately purchase it.
  • That structurally deflates our rate for reasons that have nothing to do with employer behavior.
  • Fix: exclude Enterprise from the ratio (or report it separately) before trusting any cross-company comparison.
  • Check period alignment and sample mix (geography, vertical, employer size) between our number and the benchmark's underlying sample.
  • Rule out an instrumentation or reporting error before treating the gap as real — cheapest check, do it first.

  • If a real gap remains and it's concentrated at the exposure step: A/B test CTA placement and timing — right after publish (high intent) versus a follow-up nudge.

Close with one recommendation

"Before committing to a pricing or product fix, I'd re-baseline the comparison on a matched definition first — that alone might close most of the gap. If a real gap remains after that, I'd prioritize the exposure/timing test over a pricing change, because a discovery problem is cheaper to fix and cheaper to be wrong about than a pricing problem."

The measurability standard used on this tab

Every metric below — L1, L2 and L3 alike — carries the same five fields: name, formula, grain, source signal, window. Nothing is a hand-wavy "driver". If you cannot fill all five, it is not a metric yet — it is a hypothesis.

Formula

Explicit numerator ÷ denominator. No adjectives.

Grain

What one row is: job, application, student, account, seat, school, week.

Source signal

The event or record it comes from — and whether Handshake can actually observe it.

Window

Measurement window and refresh cadence.

Two observability rules — this is the real interview trap

1. A hire is only partially observable. Handshake reliably sees applications, messages, and in-platform review actions. It sees a hire only when an employer self-reports it or an ATS integration writes the status back — which skews to large Enterprise accounts. So fill rate and time-to-fill must be defined on the observable subset, published with a coverage percentage, and paired with a fully-observable proxy (recruiter response rate) for everyone else.

2. "Qualified" needs an operational definition. Not an analyst's opinion of fit. Use "the recruiter advanced this application past first review" — then say out loud what that excludes: employers who screen entirely inside their own ATS without writing back.

Is "liquidity" actually a real, measurable metric?

Yes — it's standard vocabulary in marketplace economics (Airbnb, Uber, Etsy and DoorDash all track it), but it is never a single clean number. It only means something as the pair of L2 fill rates above, read together. Collapsing it into one blended figure is exactly what hides the redistribution problem — one side's rate rising because the other side's is falling.

Why concentration belongs on this card

Median applications per job can be flat while the distribution rots underneath: a handful of jobs absorbing everything and a growing tail at zero. Concentration and zero-application share are what make liquidity honest.

The asymmetry to name if asked to compare the two funnels

  • Recruiters pay and are the revenue side; job seekers are the scarce resource being allocated between recruiters — see "which side matters more" in Product Collab.
  • The recruiter funnel has a hard, billable end state (renewal). The job-seeker funnel has no equivalent — outcome-reported rate is the closest thing, and it's self-reported and lags by a full graduating cohort.
  • Time points in opposite directions for the same reason: slow time-to-value predicts an employer won't renew; slow time-to-first-response predicts a job seeker stops applying — same shape, opposite audience, both leading indicators of churn.
  • Retention is asymmetric in stakes, not just mechanics: a churned employer is a lost contract you can see immediately in billing; a churned job seeker is silent, and shows up only as a slow bleed in supply-side liquidity months later.

The segment trap unique to marketplaces

An aggregate can look flat while both sides degrade in opposite directions: employers receive more applications (looks like a win) while job seekers receive fewer responses (a real loss). Any segmentation of a marketplace metric should report the paired counter-metric in the same table. Say this when asked how you'd slice — it separates you from candidates who only slice one side.

Cold start and thin markets

Three distinct cold-start problems live here: a new employer with no brand or history, a new job seeker with an empty profile, and a thin market (rural school, niche major) where demand genuinely does not exist. The first two are model problems. The third is a supply-seeding problem, and no amount of ranking work will fix it — knowing the difference is the point.

Prompt style to expect

"Applications per job dropped 12% quarter over quarter — walk me through your diagnosis." Every prompt below is worked end to end. Notice that in this business the first rule-out is almost always the recruiting calendar or the denominator, not a product bug.

Open with this: the mental model that sets the tone

Say this before touching a single hypothesis — it is a verbal table of contents that signals structure rather than improvisation. Four filters, cheap and boring first:

1. Is it real?

Instrumentation, definition change, incomplete window, or a pipeline break.

2. Numerator or denominator?

In a marketplace, most "drops" in a per-job or per-user ratio are the denominator growing.

3. Season or product?

The academic recruiting calendar is a hard clock. Expected vs fixable.

4. Localize

Only now: which segment or funnel step accounts for most of the move.

If you blank, anchor on this

"Real, then numerator-vs-denominator, then season-vs-product, then localize."

The order, in six short steps
  • Legit, not broken — rule out pipeline/instrumentation first, then check the move is outside normal WoW noise, not eyeballed.
  • Seasonality — same-week-of-academic-year YoY, never raw QoQ.
  • Segment first — localize where it's concentrated before guessing why. Guessing a cause before segmenting = anchoring on the first release you find.
  • Decompose that segment — composition/mix shift vs real behaviour change (job mix, account mix, cohort mix), plus correlated component metrics (e.g. ARPU → users vs revenue).
  • Match to a candidate cause on that segment — recent release, pricing/policy change, external/competitor event.
  • Close the loop — state the mechanism as a falsifiable claim, and name what you'd measure to confirm it or prove it wrong.

Read first: sizing, rate vs continuous, and the cluster penalty

Rate / proportion metric

qualified application rate, activation, renewal

n per arm = (zα/2 + zβ)² × [p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂)] ÷ (p₁−p₂)²

Worked example: qualified application rate 22%, detect a 10% relative lift to 24.2%, α=0.05, power 80% → n ≈ 5,800 applications per arm.

Continuous metric

applications per job, time to first response

n per arm ≈ 16σ² ÷ δ² at 80% power, α=0.05. Worked example: applications per job mean 12 with SD 20, detect +2 → n ≈ 1,600 jobs per arm. Continuous metrics carry far more signal per unit, which is why they read faster.

The cluster penalty — the Handshake-specific part

Employer-side features are used by a whole recruiting team on one account, so randomization happens at the account level. That inflates the required sample by the design effect:

design effect = 1 + (m - 1) * ICC

m   = average seats (or jobs) per cluster
ICC = intra-cluster correlation

Example: m = 8 seats, ICC = 0.20
design effect = 1 + 7 * 0.20 = 2.4
=> you need 2.4x the raw unit count you calculated above
Why this matters more here than almost anywhere

There are millions of students but only thousands of Enterprise accounts. Apply a 2.4× design effect to a population of 3,000 accounts and many employer-side tests are simply not powerable in a single recruiting season. Student-level, stratified by student year and school tier. Roll out AI applicant ranking to recruiters employer-side

Employer account, not seat — a recruiting team shares a pipeline and talks to each other, so per-user assignment contaminates the cluster. Cluster standard errors at the account level and stratify by seat count and hiring volume.

Share of applications reviewed within 7 days of submission (RATE), account-clustered.

Secondary

Median time to first review, advance rate of model-ranked-top candidates, recruiter messages sent, seat utilization.

The detail — pick two or three to talk through

Fixed candidate pool: one student, one job SUTVA violation
Assumption violated

SUTVA: one unit's assignment does not affect another unit's outcome.

Why Handshake breaks it

A student accepts one offer and a requisition hires one person. If a treatment gets more applications flowing to treated employers, those applications came out of the same finite student attention and the same finite acceptance capacity that control employers were drawing on. The measured lift is part genuine creation, part transfer from the control arm — and because the control arm is depressed, the estimate is biased upward, sometimes dramatically.

How to detect it

Compare the control arm against its own pre-period trend, not just against treatment. If control fell below its baseline while treatment rose, you are looking at reallocation. Then check whether the total marketplace metric moved at all — that is the only number that cannot be faked by a transfer.

Design fix

Randomize at the market level: school × job family × season, so competition happens inside a cluster rather than across arms. Where a user-level test is unavoidable for speed, run it as a directional read and pre-commit to a cluster-level confirmation before a full rollout. Always report a total-marketplace metric alongside the per-arm effect.

Congestion: a better ranker can make outcomes worse interference
Assumption violated

That improving per-impression relevance improves aggregate welfare.

Why Handshake breaks it

If the model correctly identifies the same "best" jobs for thousands of students, they all apply to those jobs. The posting hits 800 applicants, the recruiter reviews the first 50, closes the requisition, and everyone else gets silence. Click-through rate and offline NDCG both improve. Student outcomes get worse, and the long tail of employers gets starved.

How to detect it

Application concentration (top-decile share or Gini across jobs), zero-application job share, applications per job at the 90th percentile, and the job-seeker-side response rate distribution — not its mean. A rising mean with a falling median is the signature.

Design fix

Make concentration and zero-application share hard guardrails on every relevance experiment, not nice-to-have secondaries. Consider capacity-aware ranking — down-weighting jobs that already have sufficient applicant volume — and evaluate it on student outcomes rather than on click metrics.

Seats: the account is the unit, not the user contamination
Assumption violated

Independence between randomized units.

Why Handshake breaks it

An Enterprise account has multiple recruiter seats sharing one applicant pipeline, one set of requisitions, and one team Slack channel. Randomize by user and treated recruiters will screen candidates that control recruiters would otherwise have screened, and will tell each other about the new feature. Both arms end up partially treated.

How to detect it

Check whether control-arm behaviour within mixed accounts differs from control-arm behaviour in pure-control accounts. If it does, contamination is real and the effect is diluted toward zero.

Design fix

Randomize at the employer-account level, cluster standard errors at that level, and size using the design effect 1 + (m − 1) × ICC. State the consequence honestly: with 8 seats per account and an ICC of 0.2 you need roughly 2.4× the units, which for employer-side tests is a real constraint, not a footnote.

Tiny N on the side that actually pays power
Assumption violated

Sufficient sample and an approximately normal, non-degenerate outcome distribution.

Why Handshake breaks it

There are 25M students but only a few thousand accounts carrying nearly all revenue, and contract values span $4,500 to $250,000+. One Fortune 500 renewal can swing the arm mean. Combine a small N, a heavy tail, and a cluster design effect and most employer-revenue experiments cannot reach adequate power inside one recruiting season.

How to detect it

Run the power calculation before designing anything and look at the minimum detectable effect. If the MDE is larger than any plausible business effect, the experiment is theatre — say so early rather than running it and reporting "no significant difference."

Design fix

Switch the primary to a leading behavioural rate (seat utilization, review coverage) where every account contributes signal every week. Use a binary revenue primary (renewed yes/no) or winsorize. Consider matched-pair or stratified assignment on prior-period revenue, or a switchback within account. And be willing to say the honest thing: some pricing questions should be answered with a pricing study, a structural model, or a staged rollout with monitoring — not a two-arm test.

The academic recruiting calendar is a hard clock non-stationarity
Assumption violated

A stable environment across the test window, and generalization of the effect beyond it.

Why Handshake breaks it

Fall is the internship rush, late winter and spring is full-time hiring, summer is nearly dead. Application volume, recruiter attention, and student intent all swing by multiples. A two-week test in September and the identical test in June measure genuinely different marketplaces — so an effect measured in one season does not transfer to another, even when the experiment was internally valid.

How to detect it

Plot the effect by academic week and test for a treatment × season-week interaction. Never accept a before/after comparison as evidence in this business.

Design fix

Keep arms strictly concurrent, pre-register which season the result is claimed for, and use CUPED with a pre-period covariate to strip out seasonal baseline variance. For anything shipped off-season, plan a re-validation window at peak — and state that the recommendation is season-scoped.

The outcome arrives after the decision latency + observability
Assumption violated

That the primary metric is measurable within the experiment window.

Why Handshake breaks it

Application → review → interview → offer → hire runs weeks to months, and campus offers are often made a full season before the start date. Worse, Handshake frequently never observes the hire at all unless the employer's ATS writes it back. So the metric the business cares about is both late and partially invisible.

How to detect it

Chart the cohort-aged conversion curve to see when the outcome actually lands, and chart write-back coverage over time so a plumbing change is never mistaken for a business result.

Design fix

Pre-declare a surrogate — recruiter response within 14 days is the usual choice — and validate it against historical hire data before the experiment, showing that surrogate movement has historically tracked hire movement. An unvalidated surrogate is just a convenient metric. Report the long-horizon outcome later as confirmation, on the ATS-integrated subset, with coverage stated.

Ranking experiments feed on their own output feedback loop
Assumption violated

A static treatment, and unbiased offline evaluation.

Why Handshake breaks it

Relevance models train on engagement logs the previous ranker produced. Position bias means top-ranked items get clicked because they were on top, so replaying historical logs rewards a new model for agreeing with the old one. If a model retrains mid-experiment, the treatment is no longer fixed and the estimate is uninterpretable.

How to detect it

Ask when the model last retrained relative to the test window. Check whether offline gains concentrate in high-position items. Compare offline predicted lift against realized online lift across past launches — if the gap is systematic, your offline harness has a bias problem, not a noise problem.

Design fix

Freeze model weights for the duration of the test, log serving propensities so exposure can be inverse-propensity corrected later, and use interleaving for the offline read since it is far more robust to position bias than pointwise replay. Keep an untouched holdout out of training data entirely.

Career centers are a third side that gates rollouts spillover
Assumption violated

Independence between the two sides being studied.

Why Handshake breaks it

Schools approve employers and postings, run fairs, and actively push their students toward specific employers. A change visible to career-center staff can shift behaviour for every student at that school, contaminating a student-level design. Approval gates also mean the same feature effectively launches on different dates at different schools.

How to detect it

Check whether treatment effects cluster by school beyond what individual assignment would predict, and whether school-level staff activity correlates with arm.

Design fix

Use school as the cluster whenever staff can see the change. The upside: staggered school-level approvals are a genuine natural experiment — usable for a difference-in-differences read when a clean A/B test is not possible.

The population turns over underneath you composition drift
Assumption violated

A stationary population entering the experiment.

Why Handshake breaks it

Students graduate out in waves and new cohorts are bulk-provisioned by schools on their own schedules, so week-3 entrants can be a different population from week-1 entrants. On the supply side, one ATS feed going live adds tens of thousands of jobs overnight, changing the inventory both arms are drawing from.

How to detect it

Plot arm composition (student year, school, job source) by entry week. Any drift means pooled estimates blend different populations, and a Simpson's-paradox reversal becomes possible.

Design fix

Cohort-lock the enrollment window, report results by entry cohort before pooling, and stratify assignment on school and student year so composition shifts hit both arms identically.

Fairness and exposure equity are not optional here mission guardrail
Assumption violated

That an aggregate lift is a sufficient basis for shipping.

Why Handshake breaks it

The company's stated reason for existing is access to opportunity regardless of which school you attend or who you know. Any model trained on historical recruiter behaviour will learn that recruiters favour a small set of target schools, and any ads or promotion mechanic will favour employers who can pay. Both effects are invisible in an aggregate lift and directly counter to the mission — and Handshake's institutional customers will notice before you do.

How to detect it

Impression and response share by school tier, first-generation status where available, and employer size — measured as distributions, not means. Compare the ratio of exposure to population share across arms.

Design fix

Add exposure-equity guardrails to every relevance and promotion experiment (share of impressions reaching non-target-school students, small-employer impression share) with pre-agreed thresholds that can block a ship. Raise this yourself rather than waiting to be asked — at a mission-driven company it reads as judgment, not compliance.

Evaluation: the metric ladder

1
Offline discrimination

AUC, KS, decile lift on a frozen holdout. Answers "is there signal?" — necessary, and nowhere near sufficient.

2
Task-shaped offline metrics

For ranking: recall@k, NDCG@k, MRR. For targeting under a budget: lift in the top decile. Match the metric to how the model is actually used — nobody consumes an AUC.

3
Calibration

Reliability curve and Brier score. Required the moment a score feeds a threshold, a bid, or an expected-value calculation.

4
Segment stability

Performance by cohort, tenure, school tier, employer size. A model that is strong on average and broken for new users is a broken model.

5
Online experiment on a business metric

The only step that proves impact. Everything above is a filter that decides whether the model earns an experiment slot.

6
Post-launch monitoring

Score drift, feature null rates, realized lift recomputed on a schedule. Models decay; a launch is the start of measurement, not the end.

Where the DS adds the most value in this ladder

Steps 1–3 the ML team can do without you. Steps 4, 5 and 6 are where a product analytics DS is genuinely differentiated: choosing a business metric that is measurable and real, designing an experiment that survives marketplace interference, and holding the model accountable months later. Frame your contribution there rather than competing on modelling.

Population widenedAUC dropsPrototype cohorts are cleaner than reality. Scoring all ~1M eligible users includes edge cases, sparse-history users, and cold-start users that the prototype cohort excluded.
Time has passedAUC dropsTrained on a fixed window, deployed months later into a shifted population. Without scheduled retraining this decays continuously — which is why you name quarterly retraining as a limitation up front.
Feature definitions divergedEitherThe feature store computes "28-day email engagement" slightly differently than your SQL did. Silent, and only caught by a serving-parity diff.
Different eval setEitherThey evaluated on a different holdout, a different window, or with a different positive definition. Half of all "the AUC changed" arguments are actually this.
The senior move when AUC drops

Decompose it rather than defending it. Reintroduce changes one at a time — point-in-time correction, then dropped features, then the widened population — and attribute the delta to each. That converts an argument into a table, and it usually reveals that most of the "loss" was leakage you should be glad to have removed.

And say this, because it is the real point

"AUC was the wrong final metric anyway. We weren't classifying anyone — we were ranking users to decide who to spend a limited number of prompts and sales calls on. So the metric that mattered was decile lift under a fixed budget: the top decile captured 43% of real applicants at 3× the base rate. And even that only measured ranking quality, not causal impact — proving the targeting worked required experiments, which is exactly what the five downstream tests were for."

Ranking models add two more reasons

  • Position bias. Offline replay rewards a model for reproducing the old ranker's ordering, because clicks were caused partly by position. Correct with inverse propensity weighting or evaluate by interleaving.
  • Presentation and selection effects. The candidate set the model sees online is produced by a retrieval stage that may itself have changed. A ranker evaluated on a different candidate pool is a different model.

A benchmarking framework for Relevance models

This is the deliverable the job description is describing. Treat it as a staged gate: each stage is cheap and filters what earns the next, more expensive stage.

1
A frozen benchmark set that everyone agrees on

Same queries, same candidate pools, same relevance labels, versioned and never used for training. Without this, "the new model is better" is unfalsifiable — every team evaluates on whatever data flatters them. Building this is a concrete, high-credibility first-90-days project.

2
Offline ranking metrics, plus the baseline everyone forgets

Recall@k for retrieval, NDCG@k and MRR for ranking — always against both the current production model and a trivial baseline (recency, popularity, keyword match). A complex model that barely beats popularity is a maintenance liability.

3
Bias-corrected replay or interleaving

Inverse-propensity-weighted off-policy evaluation using logged serving propensities, or interleaved comparison which is far more robust to position bias. This is the cheapest honest read available before spending an experiment slot.

4
Online experiment on a business primary

Not CTR. Responded applications per search session, qualified applications per job, or observable fill rate — with the randomization unit chosen for interference (see the previous tab), and a validated surrogate if the real outcome lands too late.

5
Guardrails with pre-agreed thresholds

Application concentration, zero-application job share, small-employer impression share, exposure equity across school tiers, apply-type mix, p95 latency, and result diversity. Agreed before the readout so nobody negotiates them afterwards.

6
Post-launch accountability

A long-run holdout kept out of the new model, plus quarterly recomputation of realized lift. This is how "our models deliver measurable business impact" stops being a slogan and becomes a number.

Why offline wins routinely fail online — know all four

Position bias: historical clicks reflect where an item was shown, so replay rewards agreement with the old ranker. Presentation bias: only items previously shown have labels, so genuinely novel good matches look wrong. Feedback loops: the model trains on the behaviour it caused, amplifying its own priors. Congestion: offline evaluation scores each query independently, so it structurally cannot see that sending 5,000 students to the same job makes everyone worse off. That last one is unique to marketplaces and is the most valuable thing you can bring to a Relevance review.

How to tell an ML team their offline win isn't a business win — without a fight

"Your NDCG gain is real and I believe it. The reason it didn't show up online is that our offline harness scores each query independently, so it can't see the congestion effect — the ranker is right about which jobs are best, and that's exactly why 5,000 students applied to the same 200 roles. Can we add a capacity-aware feature and put application concentration in the offline harness as a second objective? Then your offline metric will predict the online result, which helps you as much as it helps me."

Structure of that move: validate their work → explain the mechanism, not the failure → propose a fix to the shared measurement system → frame the benefit as mutual. Never "your model didn't work."

How to use this

Spoken answers, in your voice, grounded in the SMS propensity project. Keep the first two sentences tight — depth only if they follow up.

Tell me about a time you worked with an ML team. the core story
  • At Mailchimp, SMS penetration was stuck around 3% of about a million eligible paid customers, and we had no way to know who to target — we were waiting for customers to find the product.
  • I built a propensity model predicting whether a customer would start an SMS application: XGBoost on roughly 700 features from our customer-signature table plus custom signals like the share of a customer's contacts that had phone numbers.
  • It reached 82% AUC, and more usefully the top decile captured 43% of all real applicants at three times the base rate — so if you could only reach 10% of users, you knew which 10%.
  • SHAP turned it into product decisions rather than a score: customers who flagged SMS as important during onboarding were 20 points higher and 19,000 of them had never been contacted; propensity dropped 15 points the month after a first purchase, which meant the upsell had to happen at checkout.
  • Then I handed it to the ML team for production — and that's where I learned the most, because they cared far less about my AUC than about point-in-time correctness, reproducibility, and who owned the score.
Closing line

"The model informed who to target; it took five downstream experiments to prove that targeting actually worked. I try to be clear about which of those two things a model has earned."

What did the ML team ask you for that you hadn't thought about? credibility question
  • Point-in-time correctness on every single feature. I had one phone-contact rolling window that included same-day activity, which simply isn't available at score time — a real train-serve skew I'd have shipped.
  • Whether my train/test split was reproducible. It was, because I hashed user IDs into buckets rather than using a random seed, and that turned out to be the thing they were happiest about.
  • Where the operating threshold was and who owned moving it — I'd been thinking in deciles, which is a slide, not a product decision.
  • Calibration. With 5× weighting on positives my raw scores were biased upward: fine for ranking, wrong if anyone treated 0.7 as a probability.
  • What happens when it decays. I had no retraining schedule, and that's a maintenance commitment they were the ones inheriting.
Closing line

"The pattern was that every question was about the model's life after launch. I'd optimized for proving the signal; they had to own it forever."

How do you evaluate an ML model as a data scientist, not an ML engineer? the JD question
  • I don't try to out-model them. My job is the part they can't do alone: whether the metric they're optimizing is the metric the business needs.
  • Concretely, I'd ask four things. What decision does this score drive? What is the business metric it should move? Is that metric measurable within our decision window? And what would we have to see to not ship it?
  • Offline metrics are a filter for earning an experiment slot, never the proof. Ranking metrics answer "is the ordering better"; only an online test on a business primary answers "did anyone get hired."
  • I'd also insist on segment-level results — average performance hides a model that's broken for cold-start users, which at Handshake is every freshman and every new employer.
  • And I'd bring the marketplace-specific failure that offline evaluation cannot see: congestion. Per-query relevance can improve while student outcomes get worse.
Closing line

"So my value in a model review isn't a better loss function — it's making sure the number we're proud of is the number that matters, and that we can still measure it six months later."

How do you know a ranking change actually helped the business? marketplace depth
  • I wouldn't accept CTR or NDCG as the answer. The primary should be an outcome with reciprocity in it — applications that receive a recruiter response, not applications submitted.
  • I'd pick the randomization unit for interference, not convenience: students compete for the same finite jobs, so a student-level test partly measures a transfer from control. Market-level clusters, school by job family, are the honest design.
  • I'd put concentration and zero-application job share on as blocking guardrails, because the most likely way a "better" ranker hurts us is by funnelling everyone into the same roles.
  • Because hires land weeks later and often invisibly, I'd pre-declare a surrogate — recruiter response within 14 days — and validate it against historical hire data before the test, so it isn't just a convenient metric.
  • Then I'd hold a long-run holdout and recompute realized lift quarterly, because that's what turns a launch into measurable impact.
Closing line

"A ranking win that isn't visible in recruiter responses and doesn't survive the concentration guardrail isn't a business win yet — it's a promising offline result."

How would you push back on an ML team's chosen metric? collaboration
  • I'd start by agreeing with what's true — the offline gain is usually real, and treating it as fake is how you lose the room.
  • Then I'd explain the mechanism rather than the failure: our harness scores each query independently, so it can't see congestion or the response side of the marketplace.
  • I'd propose fixing the shared measurement system, not their model: add concentration to the offline harness, log propensities, put a capacity-aware feature on the table.
  • I'd frame the benefit mutually — if their offline metric predicts the online result, they stop getting surprised at readout, which they want more than I do.
  • And I'd bring evidence rather than an opinion: the historical gap between offline predicted lift and realized online lift across past launches. That's a table, not an argument.
Closing line

"I've found the durable version of this conversation is always 'let's make our offline metric predict the online one,' never 'your model is wrong.'"

Where does DS stop and ML start, in your view? boundaries
  • I prototype to answer whether a signal exists and is worth engineering time. That's a question about the business, and it's cheap to answer badly and expensive to skip.
  • They own serving: latency, feature availability, retraining, monitoring, and the on-call page. I don't want to own that, and pretending I could is how handoffs fail.
  • The overlap is evaluation, and that's where we should actually collaborate — jointly on the metric, the benchmark set, and the guardrails.
  • The thing I try to be useful about is the boundary itself: writing the handoff as a spec they can rebuild from, and volunteering the limitations rather than making them find them.
  • Post-launch, I'd rather own the accountability question than the model — is it still delivering, on which segments, and is it worth its maintenance cost.
Closing line

"Roughly: I prove it's worth building, they make it work forever, and we both own whether it mattered."

Phrases that signal real experience

"Point-in-time correctness"

Every feature joined as of the decision date, nothing from the future. The fastest way to sound like you've shipped.

"Train-serve skew"

Features computed differently offline than at request time. Name your own instance of it — that's the credible version.

"The offline-to-online gap"

Treats the discrepancy as a measurable, fixable property of the harness rather than bad luck.

"Operating threshold"

Where the score becomes an action, and who owns moving it. Separates a score from a decision.

"Propensity logging"

Logging serving probabilities so exposure can be corrected in later offline evaluation. Very few analysts ask for this.

"Frozen benchmark set"

Shared, versioned, never trained on. The thing that makes model comparisons falsifiable.

Things not to say

Four ways to lose credibility in this conversation
  • "The model drove $4.8M." A propensity model is predictive, not causal. Say the model informed five experiments that were projected at $4.8M, and that the experiments are what would prove it. Volunteering this distinction is one of your strongest signals — don't trade it away for a bigger number.
  • "We got 82% AUC, so it worked." AUC is a filter, not an outcome. Follow every offline number with the business metric it was supposed to move.
  • "The production AUC dropped, so they broke my model." Usually they removed leakage. Decompose the delta instead of defending the number.
  • "I'd retrain with more features." Reaching for modelling when the question is about measurement. At Handshake the constraint is rarely model capacity — it's observability, interference, and whether the metric is real.
What this case study is actually testing

Not whether you can name a model architecture. It's whether you lead with the outcome, resist reaching for AI when something simpler works, and can name how you'd know the thing is working — and failing — before it ships.

The general framework — walk every prompt through these six

1. User need

Whose problem, exactly, and what does "solved" look like for them? State this before mentioning AI at all.

2. Role of AI

What's the simplest viable approach — a rule, a filter, a template? Only reach for AI where it does something that approach genuinely can't.

3. Solution design

Where does it sit in the flow — assistive draft, ranking, retrieval? Human-in-the-loop by default; automate only where being wrong is cheap.

4. Evaluation

One offline metric that reflects quality, one online metric that reflects whether the outcome from step 1 actually happened.

5. Safeguards

Name the specific failure mode for this feature — not a generic "bias and hallucination" list — and what catches it before a user sees it.

6. Rollout

Opt-in or low-stakes first, a guardrail that must hold before expanding, and a visible way to fall back to the non-AI path.

The opening line to use on any prompt

"Before I get to the AI part — who exactly is stuck today, and what would 'unstuck' look like for them? I want to make sure AI is solving that, not just making the feature more impressive."

Adoption — measure the funnel, not "usage" reusable on any prompt
  • Exposure rate — % of eligible users who see the AI entry point.
  • Activation rate — % of exposed who click "use AI."
  • Completion rate — % of activated who generate a draft.
  • Acceptance rate — % who keep or publish the draft with only minor edits.
  • Repeat-usage rate — % who use it again on their next posting/application.
  • Share of output AI-assisted — % of all postings (or messages) created with AI.
  • Depth of use — average regenerations or prompt iterations per accepted draft.

% of eligible users who publish an AI-assisted output AND repeat it within a fixed window (e.g. their next posting within 30d). Proves the feature was useful, not just tried once.

Six worked examples, kept basic

Two employer-side, four job-seeker-side — deliberately simple versions, each run through the same six steps so the framework, not the example, is what you're rehearsing.

AI job-description assistant for employers employer-side · generation
Target business outcome

Raise posting completeness, cut time-to-publish, and increase qualified applications per posting downstream.

1 — User need

Employers writing a posting often produce vague, incomplete descriptions — missing salary, unclear requirements — which lowers response quality later. They want to publish fast without becoming copywriters.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest viable approach first: a required-field checklist alone would fix a lot of this. AI's actual value-add is going further — turning a few bullet points or an old posting into a complete, well-structured draft, not inventing requirements from nothing.

3 — Solution design

Draft-then-edit pattern: employer enters key facts (title, must-haves, salary band), a model drafts the full description, employer reviews and edits before publish. Never auto-publish an AI draft.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: description completeness score (salary present, requirements listed). Online: publish rate with AI-assist shown vs not, edit distance between draft and published version, and qualified-application rate on AI-assisted vs manual postings.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Guard against discriminatory or age/gender-coded phrasing, fabricated requirements the employer never stated, and omitted legal disclosures (state salary-transparency rules). Always show a draft, never publish automatically.

6 — Rollout

Opt-in for a subset of employers first, watch completeness and downstream qualified-application rate, then expand; keep the manual-only path available indefinitely.

Ranked-applicant AI review for recruiters employer-side · ranking
Target business outcome

More applicants reviewed per recruiter hour and faster time-to-first-review, without losing exposure parity across school tiers.

1 — User need

Recruiters facing hundreds of applicants per role can't carefully review everyone and risk missing strong non-obvious fits.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest baseline: sort by stated filters (school, GPA). AI's role is to rank the pile using signal beyond keyword filters — but it changes the order, it doesn't reject anyone automatically.

3 — Solution design

A ranking model scores and orders candidates with a short AI-generated summary per profile. The recruiter still reviews and decides; the model just changes what they see first.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: precision at top-k against historical advance decisions. Online: qualified-advance rate for top-ranked vs the rest, share of applicants reviewed at all, time to first review.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Guard against relearning historical bias (school-prestige or demographic proxies); keep the full applicant list visible and reachable; monitor exposure parity by school tier and first-generation status.

6 — Rollout

Launch as an optional sort, not the default, with an easy revert to chronological order; only promote to default after the exposure-parity guardrail holds.

AI outreach-message drafting assistant for job seekers job-seeker-side · generation
Target business outcome

Higher message/application completion for job seekers, with no drop in recruiter response rate (i.e. volume that isn't noise).

1 — User need

Job seekers often don't know how to write a compelling outreach or cover message, and blank-page paralysis lowers application completion.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest baseline: a static template library. AI's value is personalizing a draft from the job seeker's profile and the specific job, not producing a generic form letter.

3 — Solution design

Job seeker taps "draft a message," the model generates a short personalized draft from profile plus job details; job seeker edits before sending — never auto-sent.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: draft specificity (does it reference job-specific details, not boilerplate). Online: send rate and edit rate after a draft is shown, and — the metric that actually matters — recruiter response rate on AI-assisted vs self-written messages, so this isn't just adding empty volume.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Prevent generic, copy-paste-sounding messages that teach recruiters to ignore all messages; don't fabricate experience not on the job seeker's profile; rate-limit to avoid spamming recruiter inboxes at scale.

6 — Rollout

Pilot with recruiter response rate as a hard guardrail; rate-limited rollout so recruiter inbox volume doesn't spike suddenly.

Conversational job-search assistant for job seekers job-seeker-side · retrieval
Target business outcome

Lower search abandonment and higher application-start rate for job seekers with vague goals or weak filter literacy.

1 — User need

Search filters have a learning curve; a job seeker with a vague goal ("something in marketing near Chicago, part-time") often gives up rather than construct the right filters.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest baseline: better default facets. AI's role here is natural-language intent parsing into structured filters, layered on the existing ranked-search system — not a separate ranking model.

3 — Solution design

A conversational front-end parses intent into structured filters, shows the same underlying ranked results, and lets the job seeker refine conversationally; standard filters remain visible and editable at any point.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: intent-parsing accuracy against a labeled query set. Online: search abandonment rate, job-detail view rate, application-start rate for conversational vs standard search sessions.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Must not silently over-filter (e.g., misreading "no experience needed" as an exclusion); always show the filters it inferred so the job seeker can correct them; fall back gracefully instead of returning zero results on low confidence.

6 — Rollout

Ship as an alternate entry point alongside existing search, not a replacement; expand only once abandonment and application-start guardrails hold at parity or better.

AI resume optimizer for job seekers job-seeker-side · analysis + generation
Target business outcome

Higher application-to-response rate per job seeker — especially first-time applicants — without inflating application volume.

1 — User need

Job seekers don't know why they aren't getting responses — a resume that's a poor ATS-parsing fit, missing keywords from the job description, or under-quantified experience can silently tank an application before a recruiter ever reads it.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest baseline: a static checklist (length, format, contact info present). AI's value is comparing the resume against a specific job description's actual requirements and generating tailored suggestions — not a generic "resume score."

3 — Solution design

Job seeker uploads a resume and picks a target job; the model returns a gap analysis (missing skills/keywords vs the posting, formatting issues that break ATS parsers) plus 2–3 concrete edit suggestions per section. The job seeker edits their own resume — the model never rewrites it wholesale or auto-submits anything.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: keyword/requirement coverage improvement on a held-out set of resume-job pairs, human-rated suggestion relevance. Online: edit-adoption rate, and — the metric that matters — application-to-response rate on edited vs unedited resumes, same job family and season week.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Don't let the tool coach fabrication (inventing skills/experience not in the original resume) or keyword-stuffing that would look manipulative to a human reviewer; make clear this is not a guarantee of a response; don't flag non-traditional resume formats (career-switchers, non-English names) as "low quality."

6 — Rollout

Ship as an optional pre-application step, not a gate to applying; track response-rate lift on a sample before making it a default prompt on the apply flow.

AI cover-letter generator for job seekers job-seeker-side · generation
Target business outcome

Fewer abandoned applications on cover-letter-required postings, without eroding recruiter trust in cover letters as a signal.

1 — User need

Many postings request a cover letter; job seekers without one on hand either skip the application or spend disproportionate time writing a generic one, especially under application-volume pressure during peak season.

2 — Role of AI (simplest viable first)

Simplest baseline: a fill-in-the-blank template. AI's value is drafting a letter that actually references the job's stated requirements and the job seeker's profile, not just swapping in a company name.

3 — Solution design

Job seeker taps "draft a cover letter" from an application in progress; the model generates a short, job-specific draft grounded only in the job seeker's profile and the posting's text. The job seeker must review and edit before it's attached — never auto-attached to the submission.

4 — Evaluation

Offline: specificity score (cites real details from the posting and profile, not boilerplate reused across jobs). Online: edit rate before submission, and application-to-response rate for AI-assisted vs self-written letters, controlled for job family.

5 — Failure modes & safeguards

Prevent fabricated claims (experience, skills, or availability not in the profile); detect near-identical mass-generated letters sent unedited to many employers, since that is exactly the signal that erodes recruiter trust in cover letters generally; disclose AI assistance if a school or employer policy requires it.

6 — Rollout

Pilot on postings that explicitly request a cover letter; guardrail on edit rate (near-zero edit rate signals unedited mass submission — the failure mode to catch early) before wider rollout.
