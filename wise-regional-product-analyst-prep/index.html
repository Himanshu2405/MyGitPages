What Wise is

  • Mission: "money without borders" — instant, convenient, transparent, and eventually free international money movement.
  • Core wedge: the real (mid-market) exchange rate plus a small, disclosed fee, instead of banks' hidden FX markup.
  • Regulated as a money transmitter / e-money institution in every market it operates — this is why the case study calls out a "very high bar for compliance."

How the products connect (US customer's stack)

  • Entry point: almost always a one-off Personal Transfer (send money abroad).
  • Upsell 1: open a Wise Account to hold multi-currency balances instead of converting back every time.
  • Upsell 2: get the Wise Card to spend directly from those balances (avoids a second FX conversion).
  • Upsell 3: opt into Interest on the idle USD balance.
  • Separate track: Wise Business for SMBs/freelancers who get paid internationally or pay overseas suppliers.

Core customer segments

Migrants / expats (remittance senders)
  • JTBD: reliably get money to family abroad at a fair, predictable rate.
  • Price-sensitive, trust-sensitive (first transfer is the highest-anxiety moment), often recurring/habitual sends (e.g. monthly).
Digital nomads / frequent travelers
  • JTBD: spend abroad without hidden FX/ATM fees.
  • Primary product: Wise Card. Lower transfer frequency, higher card-spend frequency.
Freelancers / remote workers paid internationally
  • JTBD: receive foreign-currency payments (e.g. USD from a US client) as if they had a local bank account, avoid client-side wire fees.
  • Uses multi-currency receiving details heavily; sensitive to receive-side fees, not just send-side.
SMBs / e-commerce sellers
  • JTBD: pay overseas suppliers, receive marketplace payouts (Amazon, Etsy, etc.) in multiple currencies without losing margin to FX spread.
  • Primary product: Wise Business. Higher transaction value, lower frequency, more compliance touchpoints (business KYC/KYB).
Platform partners (banks / fintechs)
  • JTBD: offer cross-border payments to their own customers without building FX/payout infrastructure from scratch.
  • Primary product: Wise Platform/API. B2B2C — Wise's customer is the institution, the end user never sees the Wise brand.

4-step diagnosis framework

  1. Confirm it's real, not instrumentation.
    • Check event logging changes, deduping logic, timezone bucketing, a recent schema/label change.
  2. Segment the decline.
    • Platform (iOS / Android / Web), region or corridor, new vs. returning customer, funnel step (signup vs. KYC vs. first-transfer vs. repeat).
  3. Layer on time.
    • Sudden step-change (points at a release, outage, or bug) vs. gradual drift (points at a market or competitive shift).
  4. Internal vs. external cause.
    • Internal: bug, pricing change, KYC policy tightening.
    • External: FX-rate shock, competitor pricing move, regulatory change in a market, seasonality (remittance volume is seasonal — e.g. holidays).
Worked example — a real data-integrity story from the case study use this if asked for a concrete example
  • A corridor's Android + New-customer + Europe conversion rate looked like it had cratered.
  • Root cause tracing (step 2 above — segment first): a table in the raw data was headered "Europe – Android – New" but actually contained non-Android data — the word "non" was missing from the label.
  • Confirmed via the raw SQL data tab that the true Android+New+Europe numbers live in a different, correctly-computed block, and they do support the deck's real "~3%" claim.
  • Also confirmed the mislabeling never reached any customer-facing slide — the deck itself stayed clean, but it would have if the labeling bug hadn't been caught before finalizing.
  • The lesson to say out loud in an interview: before diagnosing a metric decline as a real behavioral problem, verify the segmentation labels themselves are correct — a mislabeled dimension can look exactly like a real drop.

Standard experiment design framework

  • Hypothesis: state the causal mechanism, not just "metric goes up."
  • Primary metric: pick one; add guardrails (e.g. a pricing test's primary metric might be conversion rate, guardrails = take rate, complaint rate).
  • Unit of randomization: usually customer-level for Wise (transfers are largely independent events, unlike a social/marketplace product with network effects) — but corridor-level tests need corridor-level randomization or stratification to avoid contaminating a shared FX/pricing config.
  • Sample size / power: cross-border transfers are relatively low-frequency per customer, so power calculations often need longer run times or a proxy metric (e.g. transfer-initiated rate instead of waiting for full-cycle completion).
  • Duration: account for weekly seasonality (remittance sends cluster around paydays) and avoid test windows that straddle a known seasonal spike (e.g. holiday remittances).
  • Analysis: transfer amounts are heavily skewed (a few large business transfers dominate) — variance-reduction techniques (CUPED using pre-period volume) matter more than in a typical consumer app.

Wise-specific gotchas

  • Corridor confounds: never launch a pricing test on one corridor without checking whether customers can route around it (e.g. sending to an intermediate currency first) — that would leak the treatment.
  • Regulatory review gates: some markets require compliance sign-off before a pricing or product test can launch at all — this can dominate the timeline more than the statistical design.
  • Minimal network effects: unlike a marketplace, one customer's transfer doesn't directly affect another's experience, so simple randomization mostly holds — the exception is shared FX-rate/liquidity pools within a corridor.
Example test design — tie back to the case study corridor
  • Hypothesis: lowering the take rate on the MXN→USD corridor by X bps increases conversion rate enough to grow net corridor revenue, not just volume.
  • Primary metric: corridor net revenue (not just conversion — a lower take rate that only grows volume proportionally is neutral or worse).
  • Guardrails: corridor conversion rate, cost-to-serve per transfer, complaint/support-ticket rate.
  • Randomization unit: customer, stratified by whether they've used this corridor before (new vs. repeat sender behave very differently on price sensitivity).

Practice questions

1. Monthly corridor volume with month-over-month % change

Setup: table transfers(transfer_id, customer_id, send_currency, receive_currency, send_amount_usd, created_at, status).

Ask: for each (send_currency, receive_currency) corridor, return monthly volume and MoM % change.

WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency,
         DATE_TRUNC('month', created_at) AS month,
         SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers
  WHERE status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2,3
)
SELECT *,
       (volume - LAG(volume) OVER (
          PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency ORDER BY month
        )) / LAG(volume) OVER (
          PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency ORDER BY month
        ) AS mom_pct_change
FROM monthly
ORDER BY send_currency, receive_currency, month;
2. KYC funnel conversion by step

Setup: table customer_events(customer_id, event_name, event_at) with events signup, kyc_submitted, kyc_approved, first_transfer.

Ask: conversion rate at each funnel step.

SELECT
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='signup' THEN customer_id END) AS signups,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_submitted' THEN customer_id END) AS kyc_submitted,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_approved' THEN customer_id END) AS kyc_approved,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='first_transfer' THEN customer_id END) AS activated,
  1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_approved' THEN customer_id END)
      / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_submitted' THEN customer_id END),0)
      AS kyc_pass_rate
FROM customer_events;
3. Repeat-transfer rate within 90 days (cohort)

Setup: same transfers table as Q1.

Ask: of customers whose first transfer was in a given month, what % sent a second transfer within 90 days?

WITH first_transfer AS (
  SELECT customer_id, MIN(created_at) AS first_at
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY customer_id
),
second_within_90 AS (
  SELECT f.customer_id
  FROM first_transfer f
  JOIN transfers t
    ON t.customer_id = f.customer_id
   AND t.created_at > f.first_at
   AND t.created_at <= f.first_at + INTERVAL '90 days'
   AND t.status='completed'
  GROUP BY f.customer_id
)
SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', f.first_at) AS cohort_month,
       COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_id) AS cohort_size,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) AS repeat_within_90d,
       1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_id) AS repeat_rate
FROM first_transfer f
LEFT JOIN second_within_90 s ON s.customer_id = f.customer_id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
4. Top corridors by volume with running cumulative share

Ask: rank corridors by total volume and show cumulative % of total volume (useful for a "how concentrated is demand" story).

WITH corridor_totals AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency, SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2
)
SELECT *,
       RANK() OVER (ORDER BY volume DESC) AS rank,
       SUM(volume) OVER (ORDER BY volume DESC) / SUM(volume) OVER () AS cumulative_share
FROM corridor_totals
ORDER BY volume DESC;
5. Detect a corridor's anomalous daily drop vs. its own 7-day trailing average

Ask: flag corridor-days where volume falls more than 30% below that corridor's trailing 7-day average — a lightweight anomaly-detection query, relevant to the Diagnose-a-Metric framework.

WITH daily AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency, DATE(created_at) AS day,
         SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2,3
),
with_avg AS (
  SELECT *,
         AVG(volume) OVER (
           PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency
           ORDER BY day
           ROWS BETWEEN 7 PRECEDING AND 1 PRECEDING
         ) AS trailing_7d_avg
  FROM daily
)
SELECT * FROM with_avg
WHERE volume < 0.7 * trailing_7d_avg
ORDER BY day;
6. Interest opted-in, but dormant (anti-join)

Setup: tables interest_optins(customer_id, opted_in_at) and transfers.

Ask: customers who opted into USD Interest more than 90 days ago but have zero completed transfers in that window — a churn/engagement-risk query.

SELECT i.customer_id, i.opted_in_at
FROM interest_optins i
WHERE i.opted_in_at <= CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '90 days'
  AND NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1 FROM transfers t
    WHERE t.customer_id = i.customer_id
      AND t.status = 'completed'
      AND t.created_at >= i.opted_in_at
  );

High-yield Q&A, by focus area

1. Technical Fluency & Data Integrity

Q: How do you know your numbers are right before you present them?

  • 🧩 Reconcile the same figure two ways (e.g. a raw SQL pull vs. a pre-aggregated sheet tab) before trusting either.
  • 🧩 Check dimension/segment labels literally, not just the totals — a mislabeled column can produce a real-looking but false decline (see Diagnose a Metric tab's Android example).
  • ✍️ Have your own concrete story ready — the deck's Android/"non-Android" labeling bug is a strong one if it's genuinely yours to tell; otherwise substitute a real example from your own work.

2. Technical Scoping & Demand Estimation

Q: How do you decide if a new route/feature is worth building?

  • ✅ From deck: size demand (identify → evaluate quality → estimate adoption), then weigh corridor revenue against corridor cost-to-serve, decide via payback framing — see presentation-outline.md Slide 7.

Q: How do your query/pipeline choices affect performance at scale?

  • 🧩 Filter/aggregate as early as possible (push predicates before joins), avoid re-scanning full transfer history when a partitioned/incremental read would do, watch for fan-out joins on high-cardinality dimensions like corridor × day.

3. Product Sense & Strategic Synthesis

Q: Wise keeps cutting its take rate — is that a good or bad sign?

  • ✅ From research: it's a deliberate strategy — FY26 take rate fell to 0.52% (from 0.58%), and further to 0.50% in Q1 FY27, while volume grew 31% and net revenue grew 19%. Read it as a scale flywheel (lower price → more volume → still-growing revenue), not a monetization failure, and note revenue is diversifying beyond transfer fees (interest income, card revenue).
