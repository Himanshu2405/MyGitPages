How the products connect (US customer's stack)

  • Entry point: almost always a one-off Personal Transfer (send money abroad).
  • Upsell 1: open a Wise Account to hold multi-currency balances instead of converting back every time.
  • Upsell 2: get the Wise Card to spend directly from those balances (avoids a second FX conversion).
  • Upsell 3: opt into Interest on the idle USD balance.
  • Separate track: Wise Business for SMBs/freelancers who get paid internationally or pay overseas suppliers.

  • JTBD: offer cross-border payments to their own customers without building FX/payout infrastructure from scratch.
  • Primary product: Wise Platform/API. B2B2C — Wise's customer is the institution, the end user never sees the Wise brand.

4-step diagnosis framework

  1. Confirm it's real, not instrumentation.
    • Check event logging changes, deduping logic, timezone bucketing, a recent schema/label change.
  2. Segment the decline.
    • Platform (iOS / Android / Web), region or corridor, new vs. returning customer, funnel step (signup vs. KYC vs. first-transfer vs. repeat).
  3. Layer on time.
    • Sudden step-change (points at a release, outage, or bug) vs. gradual drift (points at a market or competitive shift).
  4. Internal vs. external cause.
    • Internal: bug, pricing change, KYC policy tightening.
    • External: FX-rate shock, competitor pricing move, regulatory change in a market, seasonality (remittance volume is seasonal — e.g. holidays).
Worked example — a real data-integrity story from the case study use this if asked for a concrete example
  • A corridor's Android + New-customer + Europe conversion rate looked like it had cratered.
  • Root cause tracing (step 2 above — segment first): a table in the raw data was headered "Europe – Android – New" but actually contained non-Android data — the word "non" was missing from the label.
  • Confirmed via the raw SQL data tab that the true Android+New+Europe numbers live in a different, correctly-computed block, and they do support the deck's real "~3%" claim.
  • Also confirmed the mislabeling never reached any customer-facing slide — the deck itself stayed clean, but it would have if the labeling bug hadn't been caught before finalizing.
  • The lesson to say out loud in an interview: before diagnosing a metric decline as a real behavioral problem, verify the segmentation labels themselves are correct — a mislabeled dimension can look exactly like a real drop.

Standard experiment design framework

  • Hypothesis: state the causal mechanism, not just "metric goes up."
  • Primary metric: pick one; add guardrails (e.g. a pricing test's primary metric might be conversion rate, guardrails = take rate, complaint rate).
  • Unit of randomization: usually customer-level for Wise (transfers are largely independent events, unlike a social/marketplace product with network effects) — but corridor-level tests need corridor-level randomization or stratification to avoid contaminating a shared FX/pricing config.
  • Sample size / power: cross-border transfers are relatively low-frequency per customer, so power calculations often need longer run times or a proxy metric (e.g. transfer-initiated rate instead of waiting for full-cycle completion).
  • Duration: account for weekly seasonality (remittance sends cluster around paydays) and avoid test windows that straddle a known seasonal spike (e.g. holiday remittances).
  • Analysis: transfer amounts are heavily skewed (a few large business transfers dominate) — variance-reduction techniques (CUPED using pre-period volume) matter more than in a typical consumer app.

Wise-specific gotchas

  • Corridor confounds: never launch a pricing test on one corridor without checking whether customers can route around it (e.g. sending to an intermediate currency first) — that would leak the treatment.
  • Regulatory review gates: some markets require compliance sign-off before a pricing or product test can launch at all — this can dominate the timeline more than the statistical design.
  • Minimal network effects: unlike a marketplace, one customer's transfer doesn't directly affect another's experience, so simple randomization mostly holds — the exception is shared FX-rate/liquidity pools within a corridor.

Experiment prompts — full worked designs

Lower the take rate on the MXN→USD corridor by 10bps pricing, the case-study corridor
Hypothesis

If we lower the take rate on the MXN→USD corridor by 10bps, then corridor conversion rate and net corridor revenue both rise within one quarter, because price sensitivity is the dominant driver of drop-off at the rate-confirmation step for this sender segment.

Randomization unit

Customer-level within the corridor, stratified by new vs. repeat sender — repeat senders are far less price-elastic, so pooling them dilutes the effect. Watch for corridor-routing leakage: a customer sending via an intermediate currency to dodge the price would contaminate control.

Primary

Corridor net revenue — not conversion alone, since a rate cut that only grows volume proportionally is neutral or worse.

Secondary

Corridor conversion rate, transfers per active corridor customer, average transfer size.

Guardrails

Cost-to-serve per transfer, support/complaint ticket rate, take rate in adjacent corridors (routing-leakage check).

Sizing

Cross-border transfers are low-frequency per customer, so a full read needs roughly one remittance cycle (4–8 weeks); use transfer-initiated rate as an earlier-reading guardrail while net revenue is still noisy.

Gotchas & caveats
  • Corridor-routing leakage — a customer dodging the price via an intermediate currency contaminates the control arm.
  • Regulatory review gate: pricing changes may require compliance sign-off before launch in some markets — this can dominate the timeline more than the statistics do.
  • Transfer amounts are heavily right-skewed (a few large business transfers dominate) — use CUPED with pre-period volume for variance reduction.
Decision rule

Ship only if net corridor revenue improves or holds while conversion rises meaningfully. A rate cut that only proportionally grows volume without lifting net revenue is not a win.

Redesign KYC document upload (guided capture + auto-detection) compliance funnel
Hypothesis

If we simplify document capture with guided camera capture and auto document-type detection, then KYC pass rate rises without an increase in false-negative fraud approvals, because most current failures are blurry or mismatched uploads, not actual fraud attempts.

Randomization unit

Customer-level at KYC-submission entry, stratified by market (document rules vary by jurisdiction) and device type (camera quality varies). Analyze per-market, not pooled globally — a global average can hide a market where the change made things worse.

Primary

KYC pass rate (RATE) — approved ÷ submitted, within a fixed decision window.

Secondary

Time-to-decision, resubmission rate, first-time-pass rate.

Guardrails

Fraud/false-negative rate (approvals later flagged), manual-review queue volume, compliance sign-off status per market.

Sizing

Signup/KYC is typically the largest funnel step, so this is well-powered on a rate metric within days to a couple of weeks per market — but treat each market as its own read given differing document rules.

Gotchas & caveats
  • An "easier" submission flow that doesn't actually improve image quality can just shift failures downstream into the manual-review queue — track that volume as a guardrail, not an afterthought.
  • Most markets require compliance review before this can even launch — build that into the timeline.
  • Effect size will vary a lot by market's document rules; never report a single pooled global number.
Decision rule

Ship if KYC pass rate improves and neither the false-negative fraud rate nor the manual-review load worsens.

Prompt customers to opt into USD Interest right after their first deposit activation / retention
Hypothesis

If we prompt customers to opt into Interest immediately after their first qualifying deposit instead of leaving it a self-discovered setting, then 90-day balance retention rises, because the dominant failure mode today is "forgot the feature exists," not "evaluated it and declined."

Randomization unit

Customer-level at the moment of first qualifying deposit, excluding NY/AK residents since the feature isn't available to them — state that exclusion explicitly rather than letting it silently shrink the sample.

Primary

90-day balance retention (RATE) — still holding a qualifying balance and still opted-in at day 90.

Secondary

Opt-in rate, average balance size, time-to-opt-in.

Guardrails

Interest/FDIC-related support tickets, 7-day opt-out rate (rushed/confused opt-ins), total balance funded (avoid cannibalizing Card spend).

Sizing

Large denominator (every newly-funded customer) but the 90-day window is the binding constraint on speed — watch 7-day opt-in and 30-day retention as early reads before the full-window result is in.

Gotchas & caveats
  • Prompting right at deposit time can catch customers who funded the balance only to immediately spend it — watch for a spike in opt-ins reversed within days.
  • A competitor rate change mid-test can swamp the treatment effect entirely — track a rate-benchmark guardrail throughout.
Decision rule

Ship if 90-day retention improves without a rise in early opt-outs or interest/FDIC-related support tickets.

Issue an instant virtual card at signup, not just a physical card activation gap
Hypothesis

If new customers get an instantly-issued virtual card at signup instead of only being offered a physical card that ships in days to weeks, then 30-day card-active rate rises, because the current gap is shipping time, not a lack of interest.

Randomization unit

Customer-level at signup, stratified by market — pre-declare the slow-shipping-market subgroup as the primary read, since that's where the mechanism predicts the largest effect.

Primary

30-day card-active rate (RATE) — ≥1 transaction on virtual or physical card within 30 days of signup.

Secondary

Time-to-first-card-transaction, virtual-to-physical upgrade rate, balance-funding rate.

Guardrails

Fraud/chargeback rate on virtual cards, card-confusion support tickets, physical-card order rate (cannibalization check).

Sizing

Signup volume is large and this is a 30-day rate metric — well-powered within a few weeks globally; the real analysis is the stratified slow-shipping-market subgroup, not the pooled average.

Gotchas & caveats
  • If virtual-card use fully substitutes for physical-card orders, that could weaken the more durable physical-card relationship — track physical order rate as a real guardrail, not a curiosity.
  • A card still needs a funded balance to spend — this test really measures "discovery + funding," not card issuance in isolation.
Decision rule

Ship if 30-day active rate improves — especially in slow-shipping markets — without meaningfully cannibalizing physical card orders.

Pre-fill Wise Business signup from a business-registry API lookup KYB / business onboarding
Hypothesis

If we pre-fill business details from a registry lookup instead of requiring manual entry, then time-to-first-transfer for new Business accounts falls, because a large share of current drop-off happens during manual data entry, not during actual compliance review.

Randomization unit

Business-account level at signup, stratified by vertical/risk tier — enhanced-due-diligence verticals need review time regardless of intake speed, so the effect should concentrate in standard-risk businesses.

Primary

Time-to-first-transfer (CONTINUOUS, right-skewed — report the median, not the mean).

Secondary

Application completion rate, registry-mismatch correction rate, KYB approval rate.

Guardrails

Fraud/risk-flag rate (a bad registry match approving a business it shouldn't), manual-review volume, abandonment at the lookup step itself.

Sizing

Business signups are a far smaller population than personal ones — one of the least powerable tests here. Use a continuous/median primary rather than a binary one to extract more signal per unit, and plan for a longer run or a larger minimum detectable effect.

Gotchas & caveats
  • Registry data can be stale or mismatched, especially for newer businesses — that subset could get slower, not faster; segment it out before claiming a win.
  • Enhanced-due-diligence verticals won't benefit no matter how fast intake is — don't let a pooled average hide that this is a standard-risk-tier-only win.
Decision rule

Ship if median time-to-first-transfer falls for standard-risk businesses without a rise in fraud/risk-flag rate or lookup-step abandonment.

Practice questions

1. Monthly corridor volume with month-over-month % change

Setup: table transfers(transfer_id, customer_id, send_currency, receive_currency, send_amount_usd, created_at, status).

Ask: for each (send_currency, receive_currency) corridor, return monthly volume and MoM % change.

WITH monthly AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency,
         DATE_TRUNC('month', created_at) AS month,
         SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers
  WHERE status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2,3
)
SELECT *,
       (volume - LAG(volume) OVER (
          PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency ORDER BY month
        )) / LAG(volume) OVER (
          PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency ORDER BY month
        ) AS mom_pct_change
FROM monthly
ORDER BY send_currency, receive_currency, month;
2. KYC funnel conversion by step

Setup: table customer_events(customer_id, event_name, event_at) with events signup, kyc_submitted, kyc_approved, first_transfer.

Ask: conversion rate at each funnel step.

SELECT
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='signup' THEN customer_id END) AS signups,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_submitted' THEN customer_id END) AS kyc_submitted,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_approved' THEN customer_id END) AS kyc_approved,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='first_transfer' THEN customer_id END) AS activated,
  1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_approved' THEN customer_id END)
      / NULLIF(COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN event_name='kyc_submitted' THEN customer_id END),0)
      AS kyc_pass_rate
FROM customer_events;
3. Repeat-transfer rate within 90 days (cohort)

Setup: same transfers table as Q1.

Ask: of customers whose first transfer was in a given month, what % sent a second transfer within 90 days?

WITH first_transfer AS (
  SELECT customer_id, MIN(created_at) AS first_at
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY customer_id
),
second_within_90 AS (
  SELECT f.customer_id
  FROM first_transfer f
  JOIN transfers t
    ON t.customer_id = f.customer_id
   AND t.created_at > f.first_at
   AND t.created_at <= f.first_at + INTERVAL '90 days'
   AND t.status='completed'
  GROUP BY f.customer_id
)
SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', f.first_at) AS cohort_month,
       COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_id) AS cohort_size,
       COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) AS repeat_within_90d,
       1.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT s.customer_id) / COUNT(DISTINCT f.customer_id) AS repeat_rate
FROM first_transfer f
LEFT JOIN second_within_90 s ON s.customer_id = f.customer_id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
4. Top corridors by volume with running cumulative share

Ask: rank corridors by total volume and show cumulative % of total volume (useful for a "how concentrated is demand" story).

WITH corridor_totals AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency, SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2
)
SELECT *,
       RANK() OVER (ORDER BY volume DESC) AS rank,
       SUM(volume) OVER (ORDER BY volume DESC) / SUM(volume) OVER () AS cumulative_share
FROM corridor_totals
ORDER BY volume DESC;
5. Detect a corridor's anomalous daily drop vs. its own 7-day trailing average

Ask: flag corridor-days where volume falls more than 30% below that corridor's trailing 7-day average — a lightweight anomaly-detection query, relevant to the Diagnose-a-Metric framework.

WITH daily AS (
  SELECT send_currency, receive_currency, DATE(created_at) AS day,
         SUM(send_amount_usd) AS volume
  FROM transfers WHERE status='completed'
  GROUP BY 1,2,3
),
with_avg AS (
  SELECT *,
         AVG(volume) OVER (
           PARTITION BY send_currency, receive_currency
           ORDER BY day
           ROWS BETWEEN 7 PRECEDING AND 1 PRECEDING
         ) AS trailing_7d_avg
  FROM daily
)
SELECT * FROM with_avg
WHERE volume < 0.7 * trailing_7d_avg
ORDER BY day;
6. Interest opted-in, but dormant (anti-join)

Setup: tables interest_optins(customer_id, opted_in_at) and transfers.

Ask: customers who opted into USD Interest more than 90 days ago but have zero completed transfers in that window — a churn/engagement-risk query.

SELECT i.customer_id, i.opted_in_at
FROM interest_optins i
WHERE i.opted_in_at <= CURRENT_DATE - INTERVAL '90 days'
  AND NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1 FROM transfers t
    WHERE t.customer_id = i.customer_id
      AND t.status = 'completed'
      AND t.created_at >= i.opted_in_at
  );
